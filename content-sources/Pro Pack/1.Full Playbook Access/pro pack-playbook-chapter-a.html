A.1
What Is Physical Commodity Trading?
The difference between physical and paper markets — why a cargo is not a futures contract.
10 min
Free
Section A.1 · Chapter A
Most people think commodity trading is about predicting where prices go. The traders who actually make money know it's about something else entirely.

When a commodity trader executes a deal, they are not placing a bet on a price chart. They are arranging the physical movement of a real substance — a cargo of LNG on a vessel currently 400 nautical miles off Singapore, a parcel of crude already loaded at Ras Tanura, a batch of gasoil sitting in a tank farm in Rotterdam — from where it is to where it needs to be, at a price that makes the journey worthwhile.

This is physical trading. It involves a vessel, a loading port, a delivery window, a cargo specification, a counterparty who actually needs the molecules, and an operations team making sure the whole chain executes on time. The paper market — futures contracts on ICE or NYMEX, swaps settled against a benchmark, options on forward prices — exists alongside the physical market, but it is not the same thing. A paper position can be opened and closed in seconds. A physical cargo cannot.

The two markets are deeply connected — physical traders use paper instruments to hedge their price exposure, and paper markets are anchored to physical price assessments — but the disciplines are different. A physical trader needs to understand freight rates, cargo specifications, laytime, demurrage, and counterparty credit. A paper trader focuses more on term structure, positioning data, and derivatives mechanics. Most major commodity firms do both, but the commercial logic of the desk starts with the physical.

Physical trading is logistics with a P&L attached. Every barrel has to be somewhere, on something, going somewhere else — and the gap between what it costs to move it and what someone will pay for it when it arrives is where the money is made.

What this means for you

Whether you are entering a trading role, an analytics function, or a scheduling position, understanding the physical market is the baseline. The desk measures everything against physical reality — cargo availability, vessel positions, terminal throughput. If you can speak that language, you can follow the commercial logic that drives every other decision the desk makes.
