What is the difference between a flat price trade and a spread trade — and which one does a commodity desk actually spend most of its time on?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
May 2025

A flat price trade is a directional bet on the outright price level of a commodity — you are long or short Brent crude at $82/bbl and your P&L depends entirely on where the price goes from there. A spread trade involves simultaneously buying one instrument and selling a related one, with P&L determined by the change in the difference between them rather than the absolute price level.

In practice, most commodity desks spend far more time on spreads than on flat price. The reason is risk-adjusted return. A spread trade — say, buying the Brent/WTI spread, or buying the front-month against the six-month forward, or buying Singapore gasoil against ICE gasoil — is typically less volatile than an outright flat price position because the two legs are correlated. When crude falls, both legs usually fall together; the spread itself may barely move.

The common spread types: time spreads (M1 vs M6 in the same commodity — the carry trade); location spreads (Brent vs. WTI, JKM vs. TTF — the arbitrage trade); quality spreads (crack spreads between crude and products, or between different crude grades); and inter-commodity spreads (crude vs. natural gas, for example). Each reflects a different view about supply, demand, logistics, or refinery economics.

// The Desk Implication
The distinction matters for how you hedge. A flat price position is hedged with an equal-and-opposite flat price instrument. A spread position has its own risk profile — the hedge needs to match the spread, not just one leg of it. A trader who doesn't understand this distinction will consistently mis-hedge.

How do traders read the Platts Market on Close (MOC) window — and can participants really influence the assessment?
Former Physical Crude Trader
14 years on the desk · Singapore and London
Practitioner
Apr 2025

The MOC window — the last 30 minutes of the Platts trading day — is where the benchmark price for crude grades and refined products is assessed. During this window, market participants submit bids, offers, and done deals to Platts reporters via a regulated platform. The reporters assess what price a cargo could be bought or sold at, applying their methodology to determine which submissions are credible.

The honest answer to whether participants can influence the assessment: yes, within the rules. Submitting a high bid during the window puts upward pressure on the assessment. Submitting a large volume offer pushes it down. This is entirely legal — it is normal market participation. What regulators watch for is coordinated behaviour designed to manipulate the assessment for the purpose of benefiting an existing derivatives position. That crosses into market manipulation territory under REMIT in Europe and equivalent regimes elsewhere.

From a market intelligence perspective, what happens during the MOC window is itself a signal. If a well-known desk is consistently bidding aggressively at the MOC, it often means they are long physical and need the assessment to rise to protect their book. If large offers are appearing, they may be short or trying to sell a cargo they need to place. Reading who is doing what in the window — which requires experience and market access — is a skill that takes years to develop.

// The Desk Implication
If you are working near the front office, understand the assessment timing for every benchmark that affects your contracts. A cargo priced against Dated Brent that settles during a window where a single large participant dominated the activity should be flagged to your risk manager — even if everything was technically within the rules.

What does the crack spread actually tell you — and how does a refining desk use it differently from a crude trading desk?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Apr 2025

The crack spread is the gross refining margin — the difference between the revenue from selling refined products and the cost of the crude input. The 3-2-1 crack is the most common benchmark: three barrels of crude converted into two barrels of gasoline and one barrel of distillate. When this spread is wide, refining is profitable and refiners run hard. When it compresses, run rates fall and crude demand softens.

A crude trading desk watches the crack spread as a demand signal for crude. A widening crack means refiners are profitable — they will increase throughput, buying more crude. This tightens crude supply and supports flat price. A compressing crack signals refinery margin pressure — run rates will fall, reducing crude demand. The desk uses this to anticipate the direction of physical crude buying ahead of the refinery data confirming it.

A refining desk (or a trader hedging refinery exposure) uses crack spreads directly as a product to trade. They may sell forward crack spreads to lock in a guaranteed margin before the crude is actually processed — buying crude forward and simultaneously selling product forward at a spread that ensures profitability regardless of where flat prices move. This is the refinery hedge in its simplest form.

// The Desk Implication
Watch the 3-2-1 crack on a weekly chart alongside refinery utilisation data. When the crack compresses below $10/bbl, expect utilisation to fall within 2–3 weeks as refiners cut runs — which tightens product supply and typically reverses the crack compression. The crack is mean-reverting; the trade is timing the turn.

How does a commodity trading desk manage a position when the market is moving fast against them — what is the actual decision process?
Senior Crude Trader
11 years · Two global trading majors
Practitioner
Mar 2025

The first thing that happens is not a decision — it is a check. You verify that your position is what you think it is. In a fast-moving market, slippage, partial fills, and system lag can mean your actual exposure differs from your mental model. ETRM systems update in real time — check the position sheet before doing anything else.

The second thing is a thesis check, not a P&L check. The question is not 'how much have I lost?' The question is: 'Has anything changed that would cause me to change my thesis?' If the price has moved against you but no new information has entered the market — just volatility — and your original thesis is intact, the correct action may be to hold or even add. If new information has emerged (a supply disruption resolved faster than expected, a policy change, a large unexpected inventory build) that invalidates the original reasoning, you cut the position immediately and cleanly.

The worst outcome is paralysis — holding a position while the market continues moving against you because you are waiting for confirmation that you are wrong. That confirmation always arrives later than you would like and at a higher cost than it should. Pre-defining your stop-loss before entering the position — and committing to executing it without re-evaluation at the time — is the only reliable protection against this.

// The Desk Implication
Before entering any position, write down one sentence: 'I will cut this position if [specific event or price level].' The act of writing it forces clarity. The specific event or level is your stop. When that event occurs, you execute — you do not evaluate whether the thesis might still be valid.

What is the JKM/TTF arbitrage and how do you calculate whether it is actually open?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Mar 2025

The JKM/TTF arbitrage is the cross-basin LNG trade — moving Atlantic Basin cargoes (from the US Gulf, Trinidad, West Africa, or Norway) eastward to Asia when the Asian spot price (JKM) is high enough relative to the European hub price (TTF) to cover the incremental cost of the longer voyage.

The calculation has five components: (1) the gross spread — JKM minus TTF in $/MMBtu; (2) the incremental freight cost — the difference between the Atlantic-to-Asia route and the Atlantic-to-Europe route (typically $1.50–2.20/MMBtu for the Panama Canal route); (3) Panama Canal or Suez Canal fees if applicable; (4) additional boil-off for the longer voyage (~0.08–0.12% per day of cargo volume); (5) the cost of any cargo diversion penalty or contractual adjustment with the original buyer. If (1) minus (2) through (5) is positive, the arbitrage is open.

In practice, the window opens and closes quickly — often within days or even hours. The friction comes not from the calculation but from execution: vessel availability at the right location, flexibility clauses in the supply contract, and counterparty readiness at the Asian destination. Desks that have pre-positioned vessels and established buyer relationships at both ends of the basin are structurally faster than those that must arrange all of this in real time.

// The Desk Implication
Track the JKM/TTF spread daily alongside current LNG carrier spot rates from the BLNG index. The net arbitrage margin — spread minus freight minus canal — is the real signal. Many traders watch the spread alone and miss the fact that rising freight has already closed the window they thought was open.

How do OPEC production decisions actually flow through to physical commodity prices — and how quickly does the market price them in?
Energy Market Analyst
9 years · Buy-side commodity fund, ex-IEA
Practitioner
Feb 2025

OPEC decisions flow into physical prices through two distinct channels that operate at very different speeds. The financial channel is immediate — within minutes of an OPEC announcement, futures prices move as traders reprice their expectations of future supply. A production cut of 1 million b/d typically produces an initial flat price move of $2–4/bbl in the first hour. This is speculative repricing, not physical.

The physical channel is slower — it takes 4–8 weeks for an announced cut to actually reduce supply at loading terminals, show up in import data, and tighten physical market balances. During this lag, the market trades on the expectation of tightness without actually experiencing it. This creates a pattern where price often peaks on the announcement and then drifts lower as the market waits for physical confirmation — only to rally again when the tightness actually arrives in the data.

The complication is compliance. OPEC announcements and OPEC deliveries are consistently different things. The IEA and EIA track actual output via tanker loading data and secondary source surveys. A 1 million b/d cut announced may produce 600,000 b/d of actual reduction if some members produce above quota. Experienced traders discount the announcement and wait for the tanker data — which appears in Kpler and Vortexa within 2–3 weeks — before sizing a position on the physical tightness thesis.

// The Desk Implication
When OPEC announces a cut, the immediate futures price move reflects the announcement, not the physical reality. The tradeable signal comes 3–4 weeks later when actual loading data confirms the cut is happening. Desks that wait for the confirmation consistently enter better than those who chase the initial move.

What is the difference between DES and FOB in a physical LNG trade — and which one is better for a buyer trying to manage flexibility?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Jan 2025

In a DES (Delivered Ex-Ship) transaction, the seller arranges and pays for the vessel and delivers the cargo to the buyer's receiving terminal. Title passes at the ship's rail at the discharge port. The seller bears all shipping costs, freight risk, and cargo risk during the voyage. From the buyer's perspective, DES is simpler — they just need a receiving terminal and a payment schedule.

In an FOB (Free on Board) transaction, the seller delivers the cargo at the loading terminal and the buyer arranges the vessel from that point. Title passes at the ship's rail at the load port. The buyer bears all shipping costs and risks from loading onward. FOB is operationally more demanding for the buyer but gives them control of the vessel — and therefore the ability to divert the cargo if market conditions change.

For a buyer trying to manage flexibility, FOB is structurally superior — but only if they have vessel access and operational capability. Under FOB, if the JKM/TTF spread inverts, they can divert the cargo to Europe without needing the seller's consent. Under DES, the vessel belongs to the seller and diversion requires negotiation and often a price adjustment. The most sophisticated LNG buyers maintain a mixed portfolio — DES for baseload supply security, FOB for the proportion of volume they want to trade actively.

// The Desk Implication
When evaluating an LNG supply contract, map every cargo to its delivery basis before signing. DES volume is supply — the seller controls where it goes. FOB volume is optionality — the buyer controls where it goes. The balance between them determines how much commercial flexibility the buyer actually has, regardless of what the contract's flexibility provisions say.

How does managed money positioning in futures markets affect physical commodity prices — and should a physical trader care about the COT report?
Quantitative Analyst
7 years · Commodity-focused hedge fund
Practitioner
Jan 2025

Managed money — the category in the CFTC Commitments of Traders report that captures hedge funds, CTAs, and speculative traders — can hold positions orders of magnitude larger than the physical market's daily volume. When this community is heavily net long in crude futures, even a moderate fundamental disappointment can trigger a wave of long liquidation that overwhelms physical buying and drives flat price well below where supply-demand fundamentals suggest it should be.

Physical traders should care about the COT report for two reasons. First, crowded positioning creates vulnerability. A market where managed money is at a 52-week net long extreme is a market where any negative surprise will generate an outsized price move — not because the physical market deteriorated sharply, but because a large number of traders need to exit a large number of contracts simultaneously. Understanding this helps you size your own position correctly — a bullish physical thesis may be correct but still produce a loss if speculative liquidation overwhelms the market for weeks.

Second, extreme short positioning creates the opposite opportunity. When managed money is at a 52-week net short extreme, the physical market only needs a mild positive surprise — a slightly better EIA report, a minor supply disruption — to trigger a sharp short-covering rally. This is often the most explosive type of move in commodity markets, precisely because it is driven by forced buying rather than genuine demand improvement.

// The Desk Implication
Build the habit of reading the COT report every Friday alongside your market view. The key question is not the absolute net position — it is whether the position is near an extreme on a 52-week or 3-year basis, and whether it is growing or shrinking. A near-extreme long that is still growing is different from one that has begun to roll over.

What is a Notice of Readiness (NOR) and why does the exact timing of its tender matter so much commercially?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
May 2025

A Notice of Readiness (NOR) is a formal document issued by the vessel's master to the charterer (or their agent at the port) declaring that the ship has arrived at the port and is in all respects ready to load or discharge cargo. It is the trigger that starts the laytime clock — the contractually agreed time within which loading or discharging must be completed before demurrage begins to accrue.

The timing matters enormously because laytime is typically measured in hours, and demurrage rates on large tankers run $40,000–$80,000 per day. A dispute over whether the NOR was validly tendered 6 hours earlier or later can represent $10,000–$20,000 in demurrage exposure. The charter party specifies whether laytime commences immediately on NOR tender, after a fixed period (e.g. 6 hours), or when the vessel is in berth — and different charter parties use different language.

Common disputes arise from: the vessel arriving at the port limits but not at the agreed berth (whether NOR can be tendered before berthing depends on the 'WIBON' or 'WIPON' clause in the charter party); NOR tendered outside of business hours (relevant if the charter party specifies that laytime only counts during working hours); and NOR tendered while the vessel was not truly ready (e.g. crew documentation incomplete). Each of these is fact-specific and potentially worth significant money.

// The Desk Implication
Operations teams should maintain a real-time log of every NOR event — when it was tendered, whether it was accepted or rejected, and any ancillary communications. Reconstructing this timeline from memory six months later in a demurrage arbitration is extremely difficult. Contemporaneous records win disputes; reconstructed ones lose them.

How does a cargo nomination actually work in an LNG term contract — what are the key deadlines and what happens if you miss one?
LNG Scheduling Manager
10 years · Portfolio management, SE Asia
Practitioner
Apr 2025

In an LNG term contract, the buyer nominates a cargo by submitting vessel details — name, flag, class, IMO number, deadweight, cubic capacity — to the seller within a contractually specified timeframe before the nominated loading window. A typical schedule runs: preliminary nomination 90 days before the loading month, detailed nomination 45 days before, vessel substitution window 30 days, final loading window confirmed 15 days out.

The seller has the right to reject a vessel that doesn't meet contractual specifications — too old, wrong flag, not on the approved list, insufficient cargo containment capacity. Rejection must typically be communicated within 5 business days. If the buyer cannot substitute an acceptable vessel within the rejection response window, they may lose the lifting right for that cargo — which in a term contract can have significant commercial and relationship consequences.

Missing a nomination deadline is not automatically a loss of cargo rights — most contracts have grace periods and notification procedures. But it weakens your commercial position significantly. The seller may invoke their right to offer the cargo to a third party, or may agree to carry the cargo forward to the next lifting period at a commercial cost. In a tight market where the seller has other buyers eager for spot cargoes, missing a deadline gives them legitimate grounds to redirect supply.

// The Desk Implication
Keep a rolling 90-day nomination calendar for every term cargo, with internal deadlines set 5 days ahead of each contractual deadline. Vessel vetting in particular takes longer than people expect — P&I club confirmation, classification society records, and flag state checks can each add 2–3 days. Building buffer into your internal process protects against the external deadline.

What is the difference between a voyage charter and a time charter — and when would a trading desk choose one over the other?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Apr 2025

A voyage charter hires a specific vessel for a specific voyage — from load port A to discharge port B. The charterer pays a lump sum freight rate (often expressed in Worldscale for tankers) and the shipowner pays operating costs. The charterer's risk is limited to that one voyage. If freight rates rise after fixing, the charterer benefits — they have locked in the cheaper rate.

A time charter hires a vessel for a period — 6 months, 1 year, 3 years — at a fixed daily hire rate. The charterer directs the vessel's employment and pays voyage costs (bunkers, port costs) on top of the daily hire. The shipowner pays vessel operating costs (crew, maintenance). The charterer benefits if they can employ the vessel at revenues above the daily hire cost; they lose money if the vessel sits idle or freight rates fall below the hire rate.

A trading desk chooses a voyage charter when it needs freight for a specific cargo and has no ongoing need for vessel access. It chooses a time charter when it wants to guarantee vessel availability for a programme of cargoes, or when it has a view that freight rates will rise and wants to lock in a cheap rate now. The time charter is effectively a directional bet on freight rates alongside a logistics asset — which is why the most sophisticated commodity trading desks manage time charter books as a P&L source, not just a cost.

// The Desk Implication
When fixing a voyage charter, the freight rate you agree is the rate for that voyage regardless of what happens to the market afterwards. When taking a time charter, your P&L on that vessel for its duration depends entirely on how effectively you employ it. Know which mode you are operating in for every vessel position your desk carries.

What is a bill of lading and why does it matter beyond just being a shipping document?
Trade Operations Director
16 years · Major trading firm, crude & products
Practitioner
Mar 2025

The bill of lading (B/L) is simultaneously three things: a receipt for the cargo (confirming quantity and condition at loading), evidence of the contract of carriage between the shipper and the carrier, and most importantly, a document of title. Whoever holds an original B/L has legal title to the cargo. This is why B/Ls are issued in three originals and must be surrendered at the discharge port to release the cargo.

The title function is what makes B/Ls commercially important beyond logistics. In a string trade — where cargo changes hands multiple times during the voyage — the B/L transfers ownership each time the document is endorsed and handed over. A cargo that loaded in West Africa might be bought and sold three times during the 20-day voyage to Asia, with ownership transferring via B/L endorsements and payment against document presentation each time.

Problems arise when the B/L contains errors — incorrect volume, wrong grade specification, or wrong loading date — or when a cargo needs to be discharged before the original B/Ls have physically arrived (common when the vessel travels faster than the courier). In this case, the receiver may be asked to accept a Letter of Indemnity (LOI) — an indemnity from the seller against any loss arising from delivering without the original B/L. LOIs carry real risk and their creditworthiness is only as good as the entity providing them.

// The Desk Implication
Never accept an LOI from a counterparty whose credit standing you haven't confirmed. An LOI is only worth the financial strength of the entity backing it. For any significant cargo, your credit team should review the LOI issuer before your operations team accepts it.

How does AIS data help a scheduler manage a cargo programme — what specifically are you looking at and how often?
Cargo Scheduling Analyst
5 years · LNG portfolio management
Practitioner
Feb 2025

For a scheduler managing a live cargo programme, AIS is the closest thing to a real-time production and logistics monitor you have. I check it twice daily — once in the morning before the day's communications start, and once in the late afternoon before close. What I'm looking for: vessel position relative to expected position, speed changes, unexpected port calls, and any deviation from the filed destination.

A vessel that is significantly slower than expected raises a flag immediately — either weather, mechanical issue, or a commercial decision to slow steam (which saves bunkers but may risk missing the laycan). A vessel that has altered course unexpectedly may be responding to a cargo diversion decision at the commercial level that operations hasn't been formally notified about yet. Either way, you want to know before the counterparty tells you.

For a full cargo programme with 15–20 active vessels, I use a structured tracking sheet that pulls from Kpler or Vortexa daily and automatically flags any vessel that is more than 12 hours behind its expected position. This generates the priority list for the morning check-in — which vessel needs an update call, which loading slot may need to be renegotiated, which cargo may be at risk of missing its laycan.

// The Desk Implication
The gap between what the AIS data shows and what your counterparty has formally communicated is your operational risk exposure. If a vessel is late by 36 hours on AIS and you haven't received any formal communication from the shipowner, that silence is itself information — and the time to act is now, not when the laycan has already been missed.

What is laytime in a charter party and how does the SHEX versus SHINC distinction actually affect demurrage claims?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Jan 2025

Laytime is the period of time agreed in the charter party within which loading or discharging must be completed. It is usually expressed in hours (e.g. 72 hours allowed laytime) or as a loading rate (e.g. 20,000 MT per day, with laytime calculated from the cargo volume divided by this rate). If operations complete within the allowed laytime, no additional payment is required. If they take longer, demurrage accrues.

The SHEX (Sundays and Holidays Excepted) clause means that time spent on Sundays or recognised holidays does not count against the laytime allowance — even if loading is actively in progress. This benefits the charterer by extending the effective laytime window. SHINC (Sundays and Holidays Included) means all time counts, regardless of day — every hour the vessel is at berth counts against laytime. This benefits the shipowner.

The financial impact of this distinction can be significant. For a cargo loading over a weekend at a port where Sundays are excluded under SHEX, the charterer effectively gains 24 hours of free laytime. On a VLCC at $50,000/day demurrage rate, that is worth approximately $2,100 per excluded Sunday. For a full cargo programme over a year with dozens of SHEX vs. SHINC differences, this adds up to a material commercial variable — which is why charter party terms are negotiated carefully and why operations teams track calendar days versus laytime days separately.

// The Desk Implication
When your operations team reports that a cargo is 'running close on laytime,' always ask whether the charter party is SHEX or SHINC and what public holidays apply at that port. The same physical situation can result in either no demurrage or significant demurrage depending entirely on these clauses.

What does a cargo diversion actually involve operationally — who needs to be called and in what order?
LNG Operations Lead
12 years · Cargo management and diversion execution
Practitioner
Dec 2024

A cargo diversion is one of the most operationally intense events in commodity trading because it requires simultaneous coordination across at least five separate functions — often in three or more time zones, under time pressure. The order of calls matters because each function has dependencies on the others.

First call: the legal and contracts team, to confirm whether the existing contract allows diversion and what notification or consent requirements apply. Under a DES contract, the seller may need buyer consent. Under an FOB contract, the buyer controls the vessel and may divert without seller consent — but still needs to review the supply contract's destination restriction clauses. This takes 20–40 minutes and must happen before any other commitment is made.

Simultaneously (not after): the freight desk, to confirm vessel availability for the new route, get a freight quote, and understand the timeline. If the vessel is already loaded and at sea, the captain needs updated voyage instructions from the shipowner. Second call: the credit team, to confirm the creditworthiness of the new buyer. Third: the commercial desk at the new destination, to confirm the buyer can commit to the volume and delivery window. Finally: operations, to manage the documentation chain — updated B/L instructions, new discharge port nomination, terminal pre-arrival notification. This entire process should be completable in under 3 hours for a well-prepared desk.

// The Desk Implication
The desks that consistently capture diversion opportunities are the ones that have pre-mapped all five functions' requirements before the market moves. When a spread opens, they are verifying — not discovering — what needs to happen. Build a diversion checklist and rehearse it before you need it.

What is an ETRM system and why does understanding it matter for a non-technical role in commodity trading?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Nov 2024

An ETRM (Energy Trading and Risk Management) system is the software platform that a commodity trading firm uses to record every trade, mark positions to market, calculate risk metrics, generate invoices, and produce management reporting. Major ETRM platforms include Endur (Brady Technologies), RightAngle (ION Group), Allegro, and Openlink. Each firm typically runs one primary ETRM, though large firms may run several for different business lines.

The ETRM is the single source of truth for the desk's P&L, position, and risk exposure. Every physical trade — cargo, vessel, quality specification, delivery terms, pricing formula — is entered into the ETRM after confirmation. Every paper hedge placed on exchange or OTC is also booked. The risk management function uses the ETRM to calculate VaR, monitor position limits, and generate the daily risk report. Finance uses it to invoice counterparties and reconcile settlements.

For non-technical roles, ETRM literacy matters because it determines how quickly you can find information, how accurately you can report on positions, and how effectively you can escalate an issue. A scheduler who can navigate Endur quickly will catch a laytime calculation error in real time; one who cannot will wait for the back office report three days later. A risk analyst who understands how the ETRM marks positions will spot a mis-booked trade; one who doesn't will accept an incorrect P&L figure at face value.

// The Desk Implication
If you are entering the commodity trading industry in any non-front-office role, make ETRM system familiarity a priority. Ask in every interview which system the firm uses and whether training is provided. If it is not, look for free introductory materials from the software providers — even basic platform literacy sets you apart from candidates who have never opened one.

What is VaR and why do commodity traders often say it understates the real risk of a position?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
May 2025

Value at Risk (VaR) is a statistical estimate of the maximum loss a portfolio is expected to suffer over a specific time period at a given confidence level. A one-day 95% VaR of $5 million means the desk should not expect to lose more than $5 million on 95% of trading days — or stated differently, should expect to lose more than $5 million on approximately one trading day per month.

The reason experienced traders say VaR understates real risk is threefold. First, VaR uses historical price data to estimate future volatility — it assumes the future will resemble the past. In tail events (major supply disruptions, financial crises, geopolitical shocks), price moves can be 5–10x larger than historical data suggests. The 5% of days that VaR doesn't cover is precisely where the largest losses occur.

Second, VaR assumes positions can be exited at current market prices. In practice, a large position in a thinly traded commodity or derivative cannot be unwound quickly without moving the market against you. The exit cost — market impact — is not captured in VaR. Third, standard VaR models assume correlations between positions are stable — but in a crisis, all correlations move toward 1. Diversification benefits that VaR models as risk-reducing disappear exactly when you need them most.

// The Desk Implication
Ask any risk manager how their VaR model performed during COVID March 2020, the 2022 European energy crisis, or any other recent tail event. The answer tells you a lot about whether the firm treats VaR as a ceiling or as one input among several. Firms that treat VaR as the only risk metric will be surprised by tail events. Firms that layer stress tests and scenario analysis on top of VaR will be better prepared.

Basis risk is the risk that your hedge and your physical position don't move in perfect lockstep — leaving you with residual exposure even after hedging. It exists whenever the instrument you use to hedge is not perfectly correlated with the physical commodity you are hedging.

Concrete example: You buy a West African Bonny Light cargo priced at Dated Brent plus a differential. You hedge the flat price risk by selling ICE Brent futures. In theory you are hedged — Brent futures move with Brent. But your physical is priced against Dated Brent (a physical assessment), not ICE Brent futures (a financial contract). Normally they track closely. But if the WA Bonny Light/Dated Brent differential moves from +$0.20 to -$0.80 during the voyage, you lose $1.00/bbl on the quality differential that your ICE hedge doesn't cover at all.

Basis risk appears in four forms: quality basis (your crude is a specific grade, your futures track a different benchmark grade); location basis (your physical is in Singapore, your futures settle elsewhere); timing basis (your physical prices on a different date than your futures settlement); and instrument basis (you hedge with a swap referenced to one benchmark, your physical contract references a slightly different one). Experienced risk managers map all four for every significant position.

// The Desk Implication
When presenting a hedged position to a risk manager, disclose the basis exposures explicitly, not just the hedge ratio. '95% hedged on flat price' obscures the fact that the remaining basis exposure may be harder to price and exit than the flat price risk itself.

Firms operating European energy desks maintain compliance surveillance specifically calibrated to REMIT obligations.

What is counterparty credit risk in commodity trading and how do firms manage it day to day?

8 years · Credit function at a commodity trading major
Practitioner
Feb 2025

Counterparty credit risk is the risk that the entity on the other side of your trade fails to perform its obligations — either fails to deliver the cargo, fails to pay the invoice, or defaults on a financial instrument. In extreme cases, all outstanding positions are novated or netted to reduce exposure before a default event occurs.

What is the difference between market risk and operational risk in a commodity trading context — and which one causes more losses in practice?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Jan 2025

Market risk is the risk that price movements in the market cause losses — flat price moves, spread changes, basis shifts, freight rate changes. It is measured by VaR and related metrics and is managed through hedging, position limits, and stop-loss discipline. Market risk is the primary focus of trading desk risk management.

Operational risk is the risk of loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, systems, or human error — and from external events. In commodity trading, operational risk includes: incorrect trade booking in the ETRM (leading to incorrect hedges being placed), documentation errors in cargo paperwork (leading to delivery disputes), vessel scheduling failures (leading to demurrage), and technology failures (leading to missed market opportunities or erroneous trades).

Which causes more losses in practice is genuinely contested — but most experienced risk managers will tell you that operational risk is systematically underestimated. Market losses are visible immediately and generate rapid response. Operational losses often accumulate quietly — a demurrage claim that wasn't properly contested, a trade booked at the wrong price that wasn't caught in the evening reconciliation, a cargo that was incorrectly hedged because the ETRM booking had a wrong volume. Individually small, these losses add up to a meaningful proportion of annual P&L drag at most firms.

// The Desk Implication
The best risk managers track both categories with equal rigour. If your firm's risk reporting only focuses on market risk metrics and has no operational loss tracking or process failure reporting, that is itself a risk flag — not because operational losses are larger, but because their invisibility makes them difficult to manage.

What does it mean when a position is approaching its VaR limit — and what happens next?
Head of Market Risk
18 years · Risk leadership across multiple trading firms
Practitioner
Dec 2024

When a position approaches its VaR limit, it means the desk's current market exposure — measured at the firm's confidence level and time horizon — is close to the maximum the firm has approved for that desk or strategy. This triggers a formal review process, not an automatic action.

In practice, the risk manager will contact the responsible trader or desk head and initiate a conversation: What is the current thesis? The trader may argue for a temporary limit increase if the thesis is strong and the position is near its target. Most firms have pre-agreed protocols — if a position remains above limit for more than 24 or 48 hours without formal approval for a limit increase, automated escalation to senior management occurs. Traders who repeatedly breach limits without disclosure damage their relationship with the risk function and their career trajectory at the firm, regardless of whether the positions eventually make money.

If you are approaching a limit, tell your risk manager before they notice it themselves. The conversation is significantly easier when you initiate it with a clear thesis and a plan. The conversation is significantly harder when they call you.

What is a stop-loss and how should a trader actually think about setting one before entering a position?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Nov 2024

A stop-loss is a pre-defined price level or loss amount at which a position is automatically or intentionally reduced or closed — regardless of the current market view. Its purpose is not to predict market movements. Its purpose is to limit the damage from being wrong, by committing in advance to the maximum loss the desk is willing to accept before accepting that the thesis has failed.

Setting a stop-loss requires answering two questions before the trade is placed. First: at what price or loss level would my thesis be clearly disproven? Not uncomfortable — disproven. A Brent long based on a physical tightening thesis is not disproven by a $1.50 move against you. It may be disproven by a bullish EIA draw that produces no price response — which signals the financial market is overwhelmed by bearish positioning. The stop should be tied to the thesis, not to a round number.

Second: how much loss can I accept without this position becoming a meaningful drag on the desk's annual P&L? This is the risk-sizing question. A position sized so that a stop-loss triggers a $5 million loss on a desk with a $50 million P&L target is a very different risk profile than one that triggers a $500,000 loss. Many traders focus on the probability of being right and forget to size positions in proportion to what losing would cost.

// The Desk Implication
The stop-loss must be set before the position is entered — not while you are watching it move against you. Loss aversion and the disposition effect (the psychological tendency to hold losers too long) are most powerful precisely when you are in a losing position. Setting the stop in advance, when you are calm, is the only protection against these biases.

How does the Platts assessment process work for crude oil benchmarks like Dated Brent — and what makes it credible?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Apr 2025

Platts assesses Dated Brent — the benchmark price for North Sea crude, and indirectly for roughly two-thirds of globally traded crude oil — using its Market on Close (MOC) methodology. During the last 30 minutes of the London trading day (typically 4:00–4:30 PM GMT), participants submit bids, offers, and done deals to Platts reporters via a regulated electronic platform. Reporters review the submissions, apply their credibility criteria, and assess the price at which a BFOET cargo (Brent, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk, Troll) of 600,000 barrels loading 10–25 days forward could be traded.

The credibility of the assessment rests on three elements: transparency of methodology (Platts publishes its methodology documents and requires participants to submit real bids and offers, not indicative prices); regulatory oversight (IOSCO principles for financial benchmarks apply; UK FCA and EU BMR regulate the Platts assessment process); and market depth (Dated Brent is the most actively participated physical crude benchmark globally — enough participants submit each day that no single entity consistently dominates the assessment).

The assessment process is not perfect — it has been subject to litigation, regulatory investigation, and ongoing methodology refinements including the expansion of the BFOET basket to include US crude grades. But it remains the most widely referenced single price in global commodity markets, and understanding how it is constructed helps traders and analysts identify when assessments may diverge from actual market conditions and why.

// The Desk Implication
The methodology documents that Platts publishes for Dated Brent and other benchmarks are publicly available and worth reading carefully if your work involves contracts priced against them. Understanding the precise definition of the delivery window, cargo size, and quality specification removes ambiguity from contract interpretation and settlement disputes.

Commodity Research Analyst
9 years · Buy-side analysis, macro commodity funds
Practitioner

The CFTC publishes the Commitments of Traders report every Friday (covering positions as of the prior Tuesday), and it breaks down futures positions across four main participant categories: commercial hedgers (physical market participants — producers, processors, and merchants using futures to hedge); non-commercial (managed money) — hedge funds, CTAs, and speculative traders; swap dealers; and other reportable.

For commodity market analysis, focus primarily on the managed money net position — the difference between the total long and total short positions held by non-commercial participants. The key insight is not the absolute level but the extreme level relative to history. When managed money net longs are at or near a 3-year high in Brent or WTI, the speculative community is maximum bullish — and even a mild fundamental disappointment can trigger a significant unwind. Rising price alongside rising open interest indicates new money entering the market in the direction of the price move — a stronger signal. Falling price alongside falling open interest suggests existing long positions are being closed — less bearish than new shorts being built. The combination of price direction and open interest direction together is more informative than either alone.

// The Desk Implication
Build a weekly COT tracking spreadsheet: managed money net long/short for the 2–3 commodities most relevant to your work, tracked weekly for 2 years. The patterns you observe — how the market typically behaves when longs reach extreme levels, how long short-covering rallies last — will be more valuable than any single week's reading.

'In the past six weeks, the JKM/TTF spread has narrowed to near parity as the Australian winter maintenance season reduced Pacific Basin supply while European storage reached 85% capacity ahead of seasonal norms — which historically precedes a sharp eastward reallocation of Atlantic cargoes.' If you can open with that, you have already demonstrated more commercial intelligence than most CVs achieve in their entirety.

// The Desk Implication
The one thing that will differentiate you most from other vendor-side candidates: a documented, dated market view. Take three past situations where your data analysis led you to a specific market call, write them up as bullet points with the outcome, and include them in your resume under 'Market Views.' A practitioner reading your CV will immediately recognise someone who thinks commercially.

The Analyst-to-Trader transition is the most technically well-supported but commercially underdemonstrated career move in commodity markets. Analytics professionals have the strongest quantitative profiles of any archetype — but their commercial narrative is consistently the weakest. A model that doesn't come with a market view attached is worth less to a desk than a model that does.

What a desk wants to see before offering a commercial or trading role to an analyst: first, evidence that you have formed and held market views — not just processed data. A dated, documented market call (right or wrong, but reasoned) demonstrates commercial thinking. Not 'I built a forward curve model' but 'I built a forward curve model that showed the M3/M6 spread was underpriced given current storage levels — the desk used it to take a time spread position that generated $800K.' The commercial translation of analytical output is the skill being assessed — not the technical sophistication of the model itself.

// The Desk Implication
If you are an analyst targeting a trading role at your current firm, proactively document your market views in writing and share them with the trading team. Even if they don't act on them, the habit of forming and communicating views in a structured format — thesis, supporting signals, exit — builds the muscle you need and creates a track record that is visible to the people who make promotion decisions.

How should I negotiate my compensation when transitioning into commodity trading from another industry?
Senior Trader
17 years · Multiple commodity firms, active recruiter
Practitioner
Dec 2024

The first principle: do not negotiate on your current compensation as the anchor. Commodity trading firms pay differently from banks, consulting firms, and engineering companies — the structure is typically a lower base with a meaningful bonus that is tied to desk and individual performance. Negotiating from a banking or consulting base salary creates immediate friction because the comparison frameworks are incompatible.

Instead, anchor on market compensation for the role level you are targeting and the specific firm type. For a commercial analyst role at a mid-size trading firm in Singapore, the market base is approximately SGD 80,000–120,000 depending on experience, with first-year bonus potential of 30–60% of base in a good year. For a senior analyst with 5+ years, base ranges from SGD 120,000–180,000. These numbers vary significantly — smaller firms pay less base but higher bonus percentages; the large trading majors pay more base and higher total compensation but also have more rigorous performance benchmarks.

The second principle: the first role in commodity trading is rarely the right place to optimise compensation. The value of your first 18 months is the network, the desk experience, the ETRM access, and the track record you build — not the base salary. Candidates who negotiate aggressively on first-role compensation at the expense of accepting a slightly better-fit position consistently report regretting it. The market rewards track record more than starting salary.

// The Desk Implication
Before any compensation conversation, have a clear answer to this question: 'What would you need to see in the first 12 months to consider me for a step up?' Ask it in the offer conversation. The answer tells you whether the firm has a genuine career path for you — and shifts the negotiation from base salary to total trajectory.

What questions should I ask at the end of a commodity trading interview — and what does the answer reveal about the firm?
In-house Editorial Team
Reviewed by the Commodity Playbook editorial team
Editorial
Nov 2024

The questions you ask at the end of an interview are a signal as important as the answers you gave during it. Generic questions ('What does a typical day look like?') communicate that you didn't prepare. Sharp questions communicate that you think commercially and have done your research.

Questions that reveal something about the firm and demonstrate commercial awareness: 'How has the desk's P&L attribution split between flat price and spread trades over the past 18 months — and is that allocation intentional or market-driven?' This question shows you understand P&L attribution and have a view on how desks generate alpha. 'How does the risk function interact with the commercial team during periods of high volatility — and what is the typical timeline from risk flagging a position to the desk acting?' This shows operational and risk sophistication. 'Which market signal does the desk weight most heavily right now as a leading indicator — EIA data, freight rates, COT positioning, or something else?' This shows you understand that different market environments call for different analytical frameworks.

What the answers reveal: a desk that cannot clearly explain its P&L attribution may not have strong analytical discipline. A risk function that is described as purely reactive rather than collaborative signals a culture where risk and trading are adversarial rather than complementary. A firm that weights one signal exclusively suggests analytical rigidity. None of these are disqualifiers — but they give you information about what working there would actually be like.

// The Desk Implication
Save your best market-specific question for last. Close the interview with the market view you prepared: 'I have been following the Brent/WTI spread and my current view is [specific thesis]. Would that be the kind of analysis the desk finds useful, or is there a different angle they would approach it from?' This ends the interview on your terms — as a peer having a market conversation, not a candidate awaiting a decision.

I have been applying to commodity trading roles for six months with no success. What am I likely doing wrong — and what should I change?
Talent Acquisition Lead
10 years · Hiring for commodity trading, Singapore-based
Practitioner
Oct 2024

Six months of unsuccessful applications typically points to one of three problems, and usually more than one simultaneously. The first and most common: you are applying to the wrong roles. Commodity trading firms rarely hire externally for front-office trading positions — the vast majority of hires are in analytics, scheduling, risk, operations, and commercial support roles. If you have been applying to 'commodity trader' roles at major trading firms without prior industry experience, the rejection rate is close to 100% regardless of your background.

The second problem: your resume does not demonstrate commercial awareness of the industry. A resume that uses generic financial or analytical language — 'financial modelling,' 'stakeholder management,' 'cross-functional collaboration' — without any commodity-specific vocabulary signals that you have not engaged with the industry. Hiring managers at commodity trading firms read perhaps 20 seconds of a resume before deciding whether to continue. If those 20 seconds don't show understanding of physical markets, the rest of the resume is unlikely to be read.

The third problem: you are not building market evidence. The single most effective differentiator for external candidates is a documented engagement with the market — a written market view, an EIA analysis, a model, a blog post, anything that shows you have been thinking about commodity markets outside of the application process. This evidence can go into the resume (as a 'Market Analysis Projects' section), into a cover letter, or into a direct outreach message. Without it, you look like every other candidate who 'wants to work in commodities.'

// The Desk Implication
Change one specific thing this week: identify three commodity trading firms whose roles you want, find one person at each on LinkedIn who works in the function you are targeting, and send each a direct message with a specific market observation relevant to their business. Not a request for a coffee chat. A market observation. This approach has a 20–30% response rate in my experience — far higher than any application through a job portal.
