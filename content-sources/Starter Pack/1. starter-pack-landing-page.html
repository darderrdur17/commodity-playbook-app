Section A.1 · Free Preview

What Is Physical Commodity Trading?

Most people encounter commodity markets through price charts — a Brent crude line moving up or down on a Bloomberg terminal, a natural gas curve shifting after an EIA report. But the chart is only the shadow of the market. The market itself is a cargo somewhere on the water, a vessel fixture agreed at 11pm, a refinery bidding for a specific crude grade because its distillation unit was built for that API gravity and sulphur content.

"Physical trading is logistics with a P&L attached. Every barrel has to be somewhere, on something, going somewhere else — and the gap between what it costs to move it and what someone will pay for it when it arrives is where the money is made."

The distinction between physical and paper trading is the first thing to understand. A paper trade — a futures contract on NYMEX or ICE, a swap settled against a benchmark — is a financial instrument. It can be bought and sold, and at expiry it settles in cash. No crude changes hands. No vessel is chartered. No laytime is calculated.

A physical trade is a contract for the actual delivery of a commodity. An LNG cargo from Australia has to be nominated at a specific loading window, lifted by a specific vessel, and delivered to a specific receiving terminal. The physical trader manages not just the price risk but the operational risk — the weather delay at the terminal, the vessel arriving two days late, the buyer calling to ask about the cargo's sulphur specification...

The Energy Markets Landscape

Commodity trading is not one industry. It is four overlapping industries that share infrastructure, pricing signals, and often the same desk.

The energy commodity market is commonly discussed as if it were a single entity — "oil markets" or "energy trading" — but in practice it is four distinct markets, each with its own pricing structure, physical infrastructure, and commercial logic. Understanding how they relate to each other is essential, because the desk that trades crude oil is directly affected by what happens in the LNG market, which is directly affected by what happens in European gas, which feeds into power pricing. Nothing moves in isolation.

Crude oil and refined products form the largest and most liquid market. Crude is extracted, shipped to refineries, and converted into products — gasoline, gasoil, jet fuel, fuel oil, naphtha. Each product has its own market, its own regional pricing benchmark, and its own demand drivers. The refinery sits at the centre of this value chain, and the economics of refining — the crack spread — connect crude input costs to product revenue in real time.

LNG — liquefied natural gas — is the fastest-growing segment. Natural gas is supercooled to liquid form at -162°C, loaded onto specialised carriers, and shipped globally to markets without pipeline access. The key benchmarks are JKM (Japan Korea Marker) for Asian spot prices and TTF (Title Transfer Facility) for European gas. The spread between JKM and TTF determines whether Atlantic Basin cargoes flow east or stay in Europe — one of the most important arbitrage signals in commodity markets today.

"Energy markets are not separate silos. When Australian LNG exports drop, it affects European TTF prices, which affects coal-to-gas switching in power markets, which affects CO2 prices. Learning to trace these connections is the commercial analyst's core skill."

Pipeline gas operates differently — it cannot be rerouted the way a ship can, making it more geographically constrained but also more predictable. And power markets sit at the end of the chain, converting primary energy into electricity. Each commodity feeds into the next, and a trader who understands only one of them has an incomplete picture of the commercial landscape they are operating in.

What this means for you

When you read about a refinery outage in the US Gulf, ask what it does to product supply, then what it does to crude demand at that refinery, then what it does to the freight market for vessels that were heading there. When you read about a cold snap in Northeast Asia, trace it to LNG demand, then JKM prices, then Atlantic cargo flows. The market is always a chain of connected reactions — not isolated events.

Section A.3 · Free Preview

How a Trade Makes Money

Most people assume commodity trading profit comes from buying low and selling high on flat price. Most commodity trading profit does not come from that at all.

Flat price — the outright price of crude or a product — is one source of revenue. But for most physical commodity desks, it is not the primary one. The reason is simple: flat price is the most competed, most watched, most analysed signal in the market. Everyone has a view on where Brent is going. The real commercial edge comes from the signals that are harder to read, faster to act on, and less efficiently priced by the market.

There are six distinct levers a physical commodity desk pulls to generate P&L. Understanding all six is what separates a commercial thinker from someone who simply watches the price screen.

1. Flat price — buying a cargo at a lower price and selling it at a higher one. The simplest form, but the most competed. 2. Time spread — exploiting the difference between prices for the same commodity at different delivery dates. A market in contango rewards storage; one in backwardation rewards selling promptly. 3. Location differential — the price gap between two regions for the same commodity. Moving a cargo from where it is cheap to where it is expensive, if freight allows. 4. Quality differential — different crude grades and product specifications trade at different prices. Knowing which grade a specific refinery needs and sourcing it at the right differential is a skill in itself.

"The best trades are the ones where you are right on three levers at once — the location arb is open, the time spread is working in your favour, and you have the freight locked before anyone else. That is when you make real money."

5. Freight — the cost of moving the cargo. A desk that locks in cheap freight before the market tightens earns the differential between the rate they pay and the rate the market moves to. Freight is itself a source of P&L, not just a cost. 6. Optionality — the value of flexibility. A cargo with a flexible loading window, or a contract with destination flexibility, is worth more than a fixed one. Desks that structure optionality into their positions earn from it when market conditions change.

What this means for you

When you review a commodity trade in a case study or a market scenario, ask which of the six levers it is using — and which it is not. Most trades combine two or three levers simultaneously. Understanding which ones a desk is long or short on any given day is the foundation of commercial awareness that headhunters and hiring managers test for in interviews.
